Category 1: Fundamental / Basic Concepts
Item 1. Supervised Learning
1. Academic definition
Supervised learning is a machine-learning paradigm in which a model learns a mapping from input variables to target outputs using a dataset of labeled examples. Given a training dataset
\[
D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n},
\]

where \(x_i\) denotes an input and \(y_i\) its corresponding target, the model learns a function \(f_\theta(x)\) that minimizes an objective measuring the discrepancy between predictions and observed targets.
A typical optimization objective is
\[
\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x_i),y_i),
\]

where \(\mathcal{L}\) is a loss function. The ultimate goal is to generalize to previously unseen examples drawn from a relevant data distribution.
Supervised learning encompasses two principal task families: classification, which predicts discrete categories, and regression, which predicts continuous numerical values.
2. Supervised vs. unsupervised learning
Feature	Supervised learning	Unsupervised learning
Training data	Labeled examples	No explicitly supplied target labels
Primary objective	Predict target values	Discover patterns or structure
Typical tasks	Classification, regression	Clustering, dimensionality reduction
Example	Predicting whether an email is spam	Grouping emails by content similarity
Real-world example: Consider an email service.
- In supervised learning, a model learns from emails labeled spam or not spam and predicts the category of incoming messages.
- In unsupervised learning, a model groups emails according to similarities in vocabulary, topic, or other features without being given spam labels.
Unsupervised clustering may reveal useful patterns, but a cluster does not automatically correspond to a meaningful category such as spam.
3. Assessment exercise
A hospital has 50,000 patient records labeled with whether a patient was readmitted within 30 days. Researchers train a model to predict readmission for new patients.
Question: Is this supervised or unsupervised learning? Explain why.
Answer key: It is supervised learning because the training records contain known target labels indicating whether readmission occurred. If the target has two categories, the task is binary classification.

Item 2. Classification vs. Regression
1. Academic definitions
Classification is a supervised learning task in which a model predicts a categorical target. The categories may be binary, such as fraudulent versus legitimate, or multiclass, such as identifying one of several disease categories.
Regression is a supervised learning task in which a model predicts a numerical target, conventionally modeled as a continuous quantity, such as temperature, income, or product demand.
The distinction concerns the target being predicted, rather than the numerical format of the model's internal calculations.
2. Industry applications









Classification application 1: Financial fraud detection
A model estimates whether a transaction is fraudulent or legitimate. Its predictions may trigger further investigation or additional authentication.











Classification application 2: Medical image analysis
A model classifies an image into predefined diagnostic categories, subject to clinical validation and appropriate human oversight.











Regression application 1: Retail demand forecasting
A model predicts the expected number of units sold during a future period to support inventory planning.











Regression application 2: Property valuation
A model estimates a property's market price using features such as location, size, and property condition.

Item 3. Overfitting
1. Definition and causes
Overfitting occurs when a trained model captures patterns specific to the training data, including noise or accidental correlations, to an extent that its performance on unseen data deteriorates.
It is commonly associated with a discrepancy between training performance and validation or test performance.
Important contributing factors include:
- Excessive model complexity relative to the amount of available data.
- Noisy, mislabeled, or unrepresentative training examples.
- Insufficient regularization.
- Training for too many iterations without appropriate model-selection safeguards.
- Data leakage or inappropriate evaluation procedures, which can also make apparent generalization performance misleading.
Overfitting is not simply a model achieving high training accuracy. It is the failure to generalize reliably to relevant unseen data.
2. Visual and metric indicators

Typical indicators include:
- Training loss continues to decrease while validation loss begins to increase.
- Training accuracy remains high while validation accuracy stagnates or declines.
- A widening gap develops between training and validation performance.
- The model performs substantially worse on an appropriately held-out test set than its training results would suggest.
For example, suppose cross-entropy loss evolves as follows:
Epoch	Training loss	Validation loss
1	0.80	0.85
5	0.42	0.48
10	0.20	0.35
15	0.10	0.44
20	0.05	0.60
Validation loss reaches its minimum around epoch 10 and then increases despite continued improvement in training loss. This pattern is consistent with overfitting.
3. Mitigation strategies
Strategy 1: Regularization. Techniques such as L1/L2 penalties, dropout, data augmentation, and appropriately constrained architectures can reduce sensitivity to idiosyncratic training examples.
For L2 regularization, the objective may be written as
\[
J(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta)
+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2,
\]

where \(\lambda\) controls the strength of the penalty.
Strategy 2: Cross-validation and early stopping. Cross-validation helps estimate how well a model generalizes and supports hyperparameter selection. Early stopping terminates training when validation performance no longer improves.
Cross-validation does not itself guarantee the elimination of overfitting; it must be combined with appropriate model selection and a separate final test set when unbiased performance estimation is required.
4. Conceptual exercise
A neural network achieves 99% training accuracy but only 72% validation accuracy. What is the most plausible explanation, and what should the researcher do?
Answer key: The discrepancy suggests overfitting, although dataset shift and other evaluation problems should also be investigated. The researcher should inspect data quality and leakage, evaluate regularization and model complexity, and consider early stopping based on validation performance.

Item 4. Artificial Neural Networks
1. Structural anatomy
An artificial neural network (ANN) is a parameterized computational model composed of interconnected units organized into layers. Each unit typically computes a weighted combination of its inputs, adds a bias, and applies an activation function.
A simplified neuron is expressed as
\[
z=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_ix_i+b,
\qquad a=\phi(z),
\]

where \(x_i\) are inputs, \(w_i\) are learned weights, \(b\) is a bias, and \(\phi\) is an activation function.
The main layer types are:
- Input layer: Receives the encoded features.
- Hidden layers: Transform representations through learned parameters and nonlinear operations.
- Output layer: Produces predictions appropriate to the task, such as class probabilities or a numerical estimate.
2. Activation functions
An activation function introduces nonlinearity into the network. Without suitable nonlinear transformations, stacking ordinary affine layers would remain equivalent to a single affine transformation.
The rectified linear unit (ReLU) is defined as
\[
\operatorname{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z).
\]

For example, if a neuron receives a pre-activation value of \(-2\), ReLU outputs \(0\); if it receives \(3\), ReLU outputs \(3\).
Other activation functions include sigmoid, hyperbolic tangent, and softmax. The choice depends on the architecture and prediction objective.
3. Conceptual problem
A network has three input features, one hidden layer, and a single output neuron. The hidden neuron computes
\[
z=2x_1-x_2+0.5x_3-1.
\]

For inputs \(x_1=2\), \(x_2=1\), and \(x_3=2\), calculate its ReLU output.
Answer key:
\[
z=2(2)-1+0.5(2)-1=3.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\operatorname{ReLU}(3)=3.
\]

The hidden neuron outputs 3. The final network prediction would still depend on the subsequent layers and output transformation.

Item 5. Gradient Descent
1. Mathematical intuition
Gradient descent is an iterative optimization algorithm used to minimize a differentiable objective function. It updates model parameters in the direction opposite to the gradient, which indicates the local direction of steepest increase.
The basic update rule is
\[
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta_t),
\]

where \(\theta_t\) denotes the current parameters, \(J\) is the objective function, and \(\eta\) is the learning rate.
Intuitively, imagine moving downhill across a landscape. The gradient indicates the local uphill direction; moving in the opposite direction can reduce the objective. However, the gradient describes local behavior and does not guarantee that every step reaches a global minimum.
2. The role of the learning rate
The learning rate determines the size of each parameter update.
- Too large: Updates may overshoot a minimum, oscillate, or diverge.
- Too small: Optimization may progress very slowly and require many iterations.
- Appropriately selected: Updates may achieve stable and efficient progress, depending on the objective and optimizer.
Learning-rate schedules and adaptive optimizers can help, but they do not eliminate the need for monitoring training behavior.
3. Analytical exercise
Suppose a one-dimensional objective is
\[
J(\theta)=\theta^2.
\]

The gradient is \(2\theta\). If \(\theta_0=3\) and the learning rate is \(\eta=0.1\), calculate the next parameter value.
Answer key:
\[
\nabla J(3)=2(3)=6.
\]

Applying the update rule:
\[
\theta_1=3-0.1(6)=2.4.
\]

The objective decreases from \(J(3)=9\) to \(J(2.4)=5.76\), illustrating a successful gradient-descent step.
Category 2: Advanced Concepts
Item 6. Transformer Architecture
1. Transformer versus RNN/LSTM
The Transformer is a neural-network architecture that relies primarily on attention mechanisms rather than recurrent state updates to process sequences. Introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), it enabled highly parallelizable sequence modeling and became foundational to many modern language models.
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) process sequences sequentially, updating a hidden state as each token arrives. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks introduce gating mechanisms to improve the retention of information across longer sequences. Transformers instead use attention to establish relationships between sequence positions.
Feature	RNN/LSTM	Transformer
Sequence processing	Recurrent, generally sequential during training	Parallelizable across input positions during training
Long-range dependencies	Can be difficult to preserve	Can model direct interactions through attention
Core mechanism	Recurrent hidden states	Attention and feed-forward transformations
Computational considerations	Sequential processing can limit parallelism	Self-attention has quadratic sequence-length cost in its standard form
Typical applications	Time series, sequence modeling	Language, vision, multimodal learning
Transformers do not automatically outperform every recurrent model in every task. Performance depends on data, architecture, computational resources, and the problem being solved.
2. Self-attention mechanism
Self-attention enables each token representation to incorporate information from other relevant positions in the same sequence.
The standard scaled dot-product attention equation is
\[
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)
=
\operatorname{softmax}
\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
\]

where:
- \(Q\): query vectors, representing what each position seeks.
- \(K\): key vectors, representing information used to determine relevance.
- \(V\): value vectors, carrying information to be aggregated.
- \(d_k\): dimensionality of the key vectors.
The dot products determine compatibility between queries and keys. Softmax converts these scores into normalized attention weights, which are used to combine the value vectors.
For example, in the sentence “The animal did not cross the road because it was injured,” attention can help the representation of “it” incorporate information associated with “animal.” This illustrates a possible contextual relationship, not a guarantee that the model will always resolve the reference correctly.
3. Structural exercise
A Transformer encoder contains self-attention, feed-forward layers, residual connections, and normalization. Which component directly computes attention-weighted combinations of token representations?
Answer key: The self-attention module computes these combinations using queries, keys, and values. Positional information is also required to represent sequence order because standard self-attention alone is permutation-equivariant.

Item 7. L1 and L2 Regularization
1. Mathematical and conceptual differences
Regularization introduces a penalty into the training objective to discourage undesirable parameter configurations and improve generalization.
For a model with weights \(w\), the L1-regularized objective is
\[
J_{L1}(w)=\mathcal{L}(w)+\lambda\sum_j|w_j|,
\]

whereas the L2-regularized objective is
\[
J_{L2}(w)=\mathcal{L}(w)+\lambda\sum_jw_j^2.
\]

The exact scaling convention may differ between implementations.
L1 regularization, associated with the Lasso in linear regression, penalizes the absolute magnitude of coefficients. Its geometry and optimization behavior can produce exactly zero coefficients, resulting in sparse models.
L2 regularization, associated with Ridge regression, penalizes squared coefficients. It tends to shrink coefficients toward zero without generally setting them exactly to zero.
2. Effects on feature selection and sparsity
Property	L1 / Lasso	L2 / Ridge
Penalty	Absolute coefficient values	Squared coefficient values
Coefficient behavior	Can set coefficients exactly to zero	Generally shrinks coefficients toward zero
Feature selection	Can produce sparse solutions	Usually retains features with reduced weights
Correlated predictors	May select a subset unpredictably	Often distributes weights across correlated predictors
Typical use	Sparse models and embedded feature selection	Stabilizing estimation and controlling coefficient magnitude
Neither penalty guarantees superior generalization in all circumstances. The regularization strength should be selected using a validation procedure, and features should be evaluated within the relevant modeling context.
3. Comparative exercise
A researcher has 1,000 candidate features but expects that only a small subset is useful. Which penalty may be particularly appropriate?
Answer key: L1 regularization may be useful because it can produce sparse coefficients and perform embedded feature selection. However, its effectiveness depends on the data structure, feature correlations, and regularization strength.

Item 8. Backpropagation
1. The role of the chain rule
Backpropagation is an algorithm for efficiently computing gradients of a neural network's loss with respect to its trainable parameters. It applies the chain rule of calculus to propagate derivatives backward through the computational graph.
Suppose a network computes
\[
z=wx+b,\qquad \hat y=\sigma(z),
\]

where \(\sigma\) is a differentiable activation function. If the loss is \(L(\hat y,y)\), then
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}
\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial z}
\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}.
\]

Because \(\partial z/\partial w=x\),
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}
\sigma'(z)x.
\]

This decomposition illustrates how a gradient is assembled from local derivatives.
2. Forward and backward passes
Forward pass
1. Input features enter the network.
2. Each layer computes weighted sums and transformations.
3. The output layer generates a prediction.
4. A loss function measures the discrepancy between prediction and target.
Backward pass
1. The derivative of the loss with respect to the output is computed.
2. The chain rule propagates gradients backward through the layers.
3. Gradients for weights and biases are accumulated.
4. An optimizer uses these gradients to update parameters.
Backpropagation computes gradients; it does not itself specify the parameter-update rule. Gradient descent or another optimizer performs that update.
3. Step-by-step problem
Consider a linear model:
\[
\hat y=wx,\qquad L=\frac12(\hat y-y)^2.
\]

Let \(x=2\), \(y=5\), and \(w=1\).
Step 1: Compute the prediction.
\[
\hat y=1(2)=2.
\]

Step 2: Compute the loss.
\[
L=\frac12(2-5)^2=4.5.
\]

Step 3: Compute the gradient.
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=(\hat y-y)x=(2-5)(2)=-6.
\]

Step 4: Apply gradient descent with \(\eta=0.1\).
\[
w_{\text{new}}=1-0.1(-6)=1.6.
\]

The updated weight moves the prediction closer to the target for this example.

Item 9. Precision, Recall, and F1-Score
1. Definitions and formulas
For binary classification, the confusion matrix contains four outcomes:
- True positive (TP): A positive case correctly classified as positive.
- False positive (FP): A negative case incorrectly classified as positive.
- False negative (FN): A positive case incorrectly classified as negative.
- True negative (TN): A negative case correctly classified as negative.
Precision measures the proportion of predicted positives that are correct:
\[
\operatorname{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.
\]

Recall measures the proportion of actual positives that are identified:
\[
\operatorname{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}.
\]

The F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\[
F_1=2\frac{PR}{P+R}
=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}.
\]

These metrics evaluate different aspects of classification performance. F1 does not directly incorporate true negatives.
2. When precision or recall matters more
Precision-prioritized scenario: A system flags financial transactions for costly manual investigation. Excessive false alarms may overwhelm investigators, so high precision may be particularly important.
Recall-prioritized scenario: A screening system identifies potentially serious disease cases for further clinical examination. Missing genuine cases may be especially harmful, so high recall may be prioritized, while positive results still require appropriate clinical confirmation.
The optimal trade-off depends on the costs of errors, prevalence, and operational constraints.
3. Solved numerical problem
A classifier produces 80 true positives, 20 false positives, and 40 false negatives.
Calculate precision, recall, and F1.
Step 1: Precision
\[
P=\frac{80}{80+20}=0.80.
\]

Step 2: Recall
\[
R=\frac{80}{80+40}\approx0.667.
\]

Step 3: F1-score
\[
F_1=\frac{2(0.80)(0.667)}{0.80+0.667}
\approx0.727.
\]

Answer: Precision = 0.80; recall ≈ 0.667; F1 ≈ 0.727.

Item 10. The Vanishing Gradient Problem
1. Definition and causes
The vanishing gradient problem occurs when gradients propagated backward through a deep neural network become extremely small. As a result, parameters in earlier layers may receive negligible updates, slowing or preventing effective learning.
In a deep computational graph, the gradient often involves products of local derivatives:
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_1}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_n}
\prod_{k=2}^{n}
\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}.
\]

If many factors have magnitudes substantially below one, their product may shrink exponentially with depth.
Contributing factors include:
- Saturating activation functions, such as sigmoid in saturated regions.
- Deep networks with repeated transformations whose derivatives are small.
- Poor parameter initialization.
- Long recurrent sequences in which gradients are repeatedly propagated through time.
2. Architectural and functional solutions
ReLU activations: ReLU has a derivative of 1 for positive inputs, avoiding the small positive-region derivatives characteristic of saturated sigmoid activations. However, ReLU can produce inactive units with zero gradients for negative inputs.
Residual connections: Residual blocks provide identity or near-identity pathways through which information and gradients can propagate more directly. This can improve optimization in deep networks.
Additional techniques include suitable initialization, normalization, gated recurrent architectures, and gradient diagnostics. Gradient clipping primarily addresses exploding gradients; it is not a general solution to vanishing gradients.
3. Conceptual exercise
A deep network's early layers receive gradients close to zero, while later layers continue to learn. Identify the likely problem and explain why residual connections may help.
Answer key: The pattern is consistent with vanishing gradients. Residual connections provide alternative paths for gradient propagation, potentially reducing the extent to which gradients shrink through successive transformations.

Category 3: Applied Concepts
Item 11. Random Forest Implementation
1. Ensemble learning and bootstrap aggregating
Random Forest is an ensemble-learning algorithm that combines predictions from multiple decision trees. It uses two main sources of diversity:
Bootstrap aggregating (bagging): Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample drawn with replacement from the training dataset. Some observations may appear multiple times, while others may be omitted from a particular sample.
Random feature selection: At each split, a tree considers a randomly selected subset of candidate features rather than all available features. This reduces correlation between trees and can improve the ensemble's generalization.
For classification, the forest typically aggregates tree predictions through voting or class-probability averaging. For regression, it commonly averages numerical predictions.
2. Advantages over a single decision tree
- Variance reduction: Aggregating less-correlated trees often produces more stable predictions than relying on one tree.
- Reduced sensitivity to sampling variation: Individual tree instability can be moderated through aggregation.
- Nonlinear modeling: Random Forest can capture complex interactions without requiring a linear functional form.
- Feature importance estimates: The fitted model can provide measures of feature importance, although these measures may be biased or misleading under certain data structures.
- Practical robustness: It often performs well on structured tabular datasets with relatively little feature engineering.
Random Forests are not guaranteed to outperform every single tree or other learning algorithm, and their predictions can be difficult to interpret compared with a shallow decision tree.

Item 12. CNN vs. RNN
1. Primary application domains
A convolutional neural network (CNN) applies learned convolutional filters to local patterns in structured inputs. CNNs are widely used in image processing, spatial feature extraction, and certain time-series tasks.
A recurrent neural network (RNN) processes sequential data through recurrent state updates. RNNs and related architectures have been used for language modeling, sequence labeling, and temporal prediction.
The distinction is not absolute: CNNs can process sequences, and recurrent networks can be applied to data beyond text.
2. Core processing differences
Property	CNN	RNN
Core operation	Convolution over local neighborhoods	Recurrent transformation of hidden state
Parameter sharing	Filters are reused across positions	Recurrent parameters are reused across time steps
Parallelism	Often highly parallelizable over spatial positions	Standard recurrence is sequential across time
Typical strength	Local spatial or sequential patterns	Order-dependent sequential dynamics
Main challenges	Receptive-field limitations and spatial resolution	Long-range dependencies and gradient propagation
CNNs can expand their effective receptive field through depth, dilation, or pooling. RNN variants such as LSTMs and GRUs use gating mechanisms to improve the handling of sequential dependencies.

A research team must select an architecture for two tasks:

Answer key:

Item 13. K-Fold Cross-Validation
1. Principle and partitioning mechanics
K-fold cross-validation is a resampling procedure for estimating model performance and supporting model selection. The available data are partitioned into \(K\) approximately equal-sized folds.
The procedure follows these steps:
1. Divide the dataset into \(K\) folds.
2. Use \(K-1\) folds for training and the remaining fold for validation.
3. Repeat the procedure until each fold has served as the validation fold once.
4. Aggregate the \(K\) validation results.
If \(M_k\) denotes the evaluation metric on fold \(k\), the mean score is
\[
\bar M=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}M_k.
\]

For example, in five-fold cross-validation, the model is fitted five times, each time using approximately 80% of the data for training and 20% for validation.
2. Why it can be more informative than a single split
A single train/test split can produce a result that depends heavily on which observations happen to be selected. Cross-validation evaluates the model over multiple partitions, providing information about the sensitivity of performance to data partitioning.
However, the folds are not statistically independent because their training sets overlap. The standard deviation of fold scores should not automatically be interpreted as a conventional independent-sample standard error.
Cross-validation also requires special care with:
- Grouped observations from the same individual or institution.
- Time-series data, where random partitioning can leak future information.
- Class imbalance, where stratification may be helpful.
- Preprocessing, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning, which must occur within the appropriate training folds.
3. Solved exercise
A researcher performs five-fold cross-validation and obtains accuracy scores of 0.82, 0.85, 0.80, 0.87, and 0.86.
Calculate the mean accuracy.
Answer key:
\[
\bar M=\frac{0.82+0.85+0.80+0.87+0.86}{5}
=\frac{4.20}{5}=0.84.
\]

The mean cross-validation accuracy is 84%.
A separate final test set may still be needed for an unbiased estimate of the performance of the complete model-selection procedure.

Item 14. Transfer Learning
1. Definition and benefits
Transfer learning reuses knowledge acquired from a source task or dataset to improve learning on a related target task.
Instead of learning every parameter from scratch, a model begins with pretrained representations that may encode useful features. This can reduce training time and data requirements, although the benefits depend on the relationship between source and target distributions.
Transfer learning is especially useful when labeled target data are limited or expensive to obtain.
2. Feature extraction vs. fine-tuning
Feature extraction: The pretrained backbone is frozen, and a new task-specific prediction head is trained. Because the backbone's parameters remain fixed, training generally requires fewer trainable parameters.
Fine-tuning: Some or all pretrained parameters are updated using the target dataset. Fine-tuning can adapt representations to the new task but may require more computation and careful regularization.
Feature	Feature extraction	Fine-tuning
Backbone parameters	Frozen	Partly or fully trainable
Training cost	Usually lower	Usually higher
Adaptation capacity	Limited to the fixed representations and new head	Greater
Main risk	Insufficient adaptation	Overfitting or catastrophic forgetting
3. Computer-vision use case
Suppose a conservation organization has 1,200 labeled images of endangered animal species. Training a deep visual model from scratch may be inefficient.
The organization could start with an image model pretrained on a large dataset, replace its final classification layer, and train the new layer on the animal images. If the target domain differs substantially from the source domain, selected backbone layers could subsequently be fine-tuned.
Evaluation should use a properly separated test set, with particular attention to class imbalance and potential overlap between training and test images.
4. Conceptual exercise
A dataset contains only 500 labeled examples. The pretrained model already recognizes general visual patterns. Which approach should be tried first?
Answer key: Feature extraction is a reasonable baseline because it reduces the number of trainable parameters. Fine-tuning should then be evaluated as a competing approach rather than assumed to be superior.

Item 15. Batch, Stochastic, and Mini-batch Gradient Descent
1. Definitions
Batch gradient descent computes the gradient using the entire training dataset for each parameter update.
Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) uses one randomly selected training example per update.
Mini-batch gradient descent calculates each update using a subset of training examples.
For a mini-batch \(B_t\), the update is
\[
\theta_{t+1}
=
\theta_t-\eta
\frac{1}{|B_t|}
\sum_{i\in B_t}
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_i(\theta_t).
\]

2. Comparative summary
Property	Batch	Stochastic	Mini-batch
Examples per update	Entire dataset	One example	A subset
Gradient noise	Usually lowest	Highest	Intermediate
Update frequency	Low	High	High
Memory requirement	Can be substantial	Usually low per update	Adjustable
Parallel computation	Possible, but large-batch processing may be costly	Limited per update	Well suited to vectorized hardware
Optimization behavior	Smooth updates	Noisy trajectory	Balances efficiency and gradient variability
3. Why mini-batch training is widely used
Mini-batch methods provide a practical balance between computational efficiency and the variability of stochastic updates. They support efficient matrix operations on GPUs and other accelerators, allow control over memory consumption, and provide frequent parameter updates.
Nevertheless, mini-batch gradient descent is not universally optimal. Batch size, learning rate, data characteristics, hardware, and optimization objectives all influence performance.
4. Comparative exercise
A training dataset contains 100,000 examples. A model uses batches of 500 examples.
How many parameter updates are performed during one complete epoch, assuming every example is used exactly once?
Answer key:
\[
\frac{100{,}000}{500}=200.
\]

The model performs 200 updates per epoch.

Category 4: Socio-Technical & Ethical Concepts
Item 16. AI Ethics and Algorithmic Fairness
1. Definition and real-world illustration
AI ethics examines the moral, social, and institutional implications of designing, deploying, and governing artificial intelligence systems. Algorithmic fairness concerns whether the outcomes and decision processes of such systems treat relevant individuals and groups equitably under an explicitly defined fairness criterion.
Algorithmic bias refers to systematic patterns in data, model behavior, or deployment practices that produce inaccurate or inequitable outcomes. Bias can originate from historical inequalities in training data, measurement errors, unrepresentative samples, proxy variables, or feedback loops.
Consider an automated hiring system trained on historical recruitment decisions. If previous hiring patterns systematically favored one demographic group, a model trained to reproduce those decisions may learn to disadvantage other groups, even without explicitly receiving demographic identity as an input.
The example illustrates a central problem: predicting historical decisions accurately is not necessarily equivalent to making fair decisions.
2. Distributive and procedural justice
Distributive justice concerns how benefits, opportunities, risks, and burdens are allocated. In an educational AI system, for example, one group of students might receive less accurate feedback than another because of differences in language representation.
Procedural justice concerns the fairness and legitimacy of the decision-making process. Relevant considerations include transparency, the opportunity to contest decisions, consistency, accountability, and access to meaningful appeals.
These dimensions can conflict. A system may distribute outcomes more equally under one metric while limiting individual explanations or appeals. Moreover, fairness criteria can be mathematically incompatible under certain statistical conditions, particularly when group base rates differ and prediction errors are nonzero.
Consequently, fairness must be specified in relation to the application, affected groups, and consequences of errors rather than reduced to a single universal metric.
3. Discussion scenario
A university introduces an AI system that predicts which students are at risk of failing a course. The system identifies students from one linguistic minority substantially more often than other students.
Discuss:
1. What evidence would establish whether the difference reflects actual educational needs or model bias?
2. Which fairness measures should the university consider?
3. How should students challenge an incorrect prediction?
4. Should the system be permitted to influence access to tutoring or academic opportunities?
Suggested answer: The university should examine subgroup error rates, data representativeness, calibration, and the validity of the target labels. It should investigate whether historical data reflect unequal opportunities rather than genuine differences in ability. Students should have access to human review, and the system should support assistance rather than automatically restrict educational opportunities.

Item 17. Digital Sanctions and AI Infrastructure Access
1. Infrastructure and API access barriers
Access to AI research infrastructure depends on more than the availability of model weights or published research papers. Researchers may require cloud computing, model-hosting services, application programming interfaces (APIs), payment systems, software repositories, and reliable internet connectivity.
In regions affected by international sanctions or other restrictions, access may be constrained by legal requirements, provider policies, payment limitations, identity verification, or service availability. These barriers are not identical across countries, institutions, or providers.
Researchers may also face non-sanctions-related obstacles, including limited computing budgets, expensive hardware, inadequate bandwidth, and insufficient access to specialized datasets.
These conditions can create research inequalities when some institutions can repeatedly evaluate frontier models while others cannot reproduce the same experiments.
2. Open-source and community-driven alternatives
Several approaches may reduce dependence on proprietary infrastructure:
- Open-weight models: Models whose weights are available under specified terms can support local inference and, in some cases, fine-tuning.
- Local deployment: Running models on institutionally controlled hardware can reduce dependence on external APIs, subject to hardware and licensing constraints.
- Shared computing infrastructure: Research consortia and university clusters can pool resources.
- Open datasets and reproducible benchmarks: Publicly documented evaluation datasets and code can improve methodological transparency.
- Community collaboration: Cross-institutional research can support replication, language-resource development, and comparative evaluation.
Open weights do not necessarily imply unrestricted licensing, complete reproducibility, or equivalent performance to a proprietary frontier model. Researchers must check applicable licenses, export controls, data-protection requirements, and institutional policies.
3. Critical reflection exercise
A researcher at a university with restricted API access cannot reproduce an experiment that depends on a proprietary LLM.
Should the researcher replace the model with an open-weight alternative, request institutional collaboration, or abandon the experiment?
Suggested answer: The decision depends on the research question. If the study evaluates a specific proprietary model, replacing it changes the target of inference and should not be treated as an exact replication. Institutional collaboration may preserve the original design where legally and practically feasible. An open-weight model can support a related robustness or comparative study, provided the change is explicitly documented.
Researchers should distinguish exact replication from conceptual replication and report infrastructure limitations transparently.

Item 18. Federated Learning
1. Operational mechanism
Federated learning is a distributed machine-learning paradigm in which multiple participants collaboratively train a shared model while keeping their raw training data on their local devices or institutional systems.
A common implementation, Federated Averaging (FedAvg), follows this procedure:
1. A coordinating server distributes the current global model to selected participants.
2. Each participant trains the model locally using its own data.
3. Participants transmit model updates or parameter differences rather than raw examples.
4. The server aggregates the updates, often using a weighted average.
5. The updated global model is redistributed for subsequent training rounds.
A simplified aggregation rule is
\[
w_{t+1}
=
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{n_k}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_j}
w_{t+1}^{(k)},
\]

where \(w_{t+1}^{(k)}\) represents participant \(k\)'s locally updated parameters and \(n_k\) is its number of training examples.
2. Privacy and governance
Keeping raw data local can reduce the need to centralize sensitive records. However, federated learning does not automatically guarantee privacy: model updates can leak information about local training examples.
Additional safeguards may include:
- Differential privacy: Introduces controlled randomness to bound what can be inferred about an individual's contribution, with a measurable privacy–utility trade-off.
- Secure aggregation: Allows a server to obtain an aggregate of participant updates without directly inspecting each individual update.
- Access controls and audit logs: Restrict access and support governance of model updates.
- Data minimization and retention limits: Reduce unnecessary exposure of sensitive information.
These safeguards address different threats and may be combined.
3. Practical use case
Several hospitals want to train a model that predicts patient deterioration. Each hospital holds sensitive patient records and cannot freely transfer them to a central research database.
Under a federated approach, participating hospitals train local model updates and contribute to a shared model. Independent evaluation remains necessary to assess performance across institutions, including differences in patient populations and clinical practice.
4. Conceptual exercise
A hospital participates in federated learning and never sends raw patient records to the central server. Can it conclude that patient privacy is guaranteed?
Answer key: No. Model updates may still reveal information, and privacy also depends on system design, adversary capabilities, access controls, and governance. Appropriate technical and organizational safeguards are required.

Item 19. Explainability vs. Accuracy in AI
1. The black-box problem
Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) encompasses methods intended to make model behavior more understandable to humans. The broader goal is to help users inspect predictions, identify influential features, evaluate limitations, and support appropriate accountability.
Complex models, including deep neural networks, can learn intricate representations involving millions or billions of parameters. Although these models are computationally explicit, their internal operations may not translate into explanations that humans can readily interpret.
Methods such as feature attribution, counterfactual explanations, saliency maps, and interpretable surrogate models can provide useful insights. However, explanations may be incomplete, unstable, or misleading if their assumptions are not examined.
An explanation that sounds plausible is not necessarily a faithful description of the model's actual reasoning.
2. Interpretability versus predictive performance
In high-stakes domains, users may need to understand why a model generated a prediction, whether its output is reliable, and how to challenge an adverse decision.
For example, in healthcare, a model that predicts disease risk may need to provide clinically meaningful evidence and undergo external validation. In criminal justice, a risk-assessment tool raises questions about contestability, bias, proportionality, and the legitimacy of using particular predictors.
Interpretability and predictive accuracy are not necessarily opposing objectives. Some interpretable models perform competitively, while complex models may provide greater predictive performance in certain settings. The appropriate choice depends on the consequences of errors, the quality of the available explanations, and the needs of the people affected.
3. Debate exercise
A hospital must choose between two predictive systems:
- Model A achieves 94% accuracy but provides limited explanations.
- Model B achieves 91% accuracy and provides clinically interpretable predictions.
Which model should the hospital deploy?
Suggested answer: The reported accuracy figures alone are insufficient. The hospital should examine external validation, sensitivity and specificity, calibration, subgroup performance, error severity, clinical utility, and the fidelity of Model B's explanations. If Model B offers meaningful interpretability with acceptable clinical performance, it may be preferable in a setting where clinicians must scrutinize and justify decisions. However, the final decision requires evidence about actual clinical outcomes and the consequences of each model's errors.

Item 20. Generative AI and the Transformation of Educators' Roles
1. From knowledge transmission to learning design
Generative AI can produce explanations, examples, exercises, summaries, and personalized feedback. These capabilities may change the educator's role from primarily transmitting information toward designing learning experiences, facilitating inquiry, evaluating evidence, and helping students develop critical judgment.
However, this is not a complete replacement of traditional teaching. Educators remain responsible for curricular alignment, conceptual accuracy, appropriate scaffolding, assessment validity, and the social dimensions of learning.
AI-generated content should be treated as a resource to evaluate rather than an authoritative substitute for disciplinary expertise.
2. Opportunities and pedagogical risks
Potential opportunities
- Faster production of differentiated learning materials.
- More opportunities for formative feedback.
- Interactive explanations and practice exercises.
- Support for multilingual learners.
- Assistance with lesson planning and resource adaptation.
Potential risks
- Hallucinated facts and misleading explanations.
- Overreliance on generated answers.
- Reduced student engagement in independent reasoning.
- Unequal access to capable models and reliable infrastructure.
- Privacy and intellectual-property concerns.
- Assessment contamination when AI use is inconsistent with assessment objectives.
- Linguistic or cultural bias in generated instructional content.
The appropriate response is neither unconditional adoption nor blanket rejection. Educational integration should be guided by learning objectives, transparent rules, appropriate verification, and evidence of instructional value.
3. A pedagogical integration framework
A practical framework can be organized into five stages.

Stage 1: Define learning objectives
Identify the knowledge, skills, and reasoning processes students should demonstrate independently of AI.




Stage 2: Generate learning resources
Use AI to draft explanations, exercises, alternative examples, or formative feedback aligned with the objectives.




Stage 3: Verify and adapt
Check factual accuracy, educational appropriateness, linguistic accessibility, and cultural relevance before using consequential materials.




Stage 4: Facilitate active learning
Ask students to compare explanations, justify answers, identify errors, and reflect on the strengths and limitations of AI-generated content.




Stage 5: Evaluate and improve
Assess learning outcomes, review student feedback, document errors, and revise the instructional workflow based on evidence.



4. Proposed pedagogical integration scenario
An instructor uses an LLM to create a lesson on neural networks. The generated explanation contains an inaccurate statement about activation functions.
Rather than simply correcting the text and distributing it, the instructor asks students to identify the error, explain why it is incorrect, and verify their conclusions against authoritative course materials.
This approach uses AI both as a resource for instruction and as an opportunity to develop critical AI literacy. The educator remains responsible for ensuring that students receive accurate explanations and appropriate feedback.
5. Assessment exercise
A university allows students to use generative AI when preparing formative assignments but requires independent work during final examinations.
What principles should guide the policy?
Suggested answer: The policy should align AI permissions with the intended learning outcomes, distinguish formative assistance from independent assessment, communicate acceptable use clearly, protect student privacy, and provide equitable access. Assessments should evaluate the competencies students are expected to demonstrate, with transparent procedures for disclosing AI assistance where required.
